When you design a social network the choice of data structure is critical to accurately model out relationships and ensure the most efficient operations. A graph would be best in this scenario due to how graphs can capture the complexity of human connections. This is great because many relationships we have are many to many, reciprocal, and dynamic. A social network is a set of entities and relationships, this would be best for a graph model due to how a graph allows bidirectional and directed relationships. If we wanted to represent the strength of a connection we can show it due to how flexible a graph is.

Trees and lists tend to fall apart due to how trees and lists function. A list is very linear, while it can store people it cannot efficiently represent the multiple overlapping relationships that exist within a social network. To find a person’s friends you would need to scan the entire list to find them. This is very inefficient and it does not represent the relational structure of that person’s friends. A tree enforces strict parent-child hierarchy. This only works best for family trees or organizational charts. It is inefficient due to how cycles and multiple connections are common. A tree cannot represent a relationship between alex and jordan and taylor as alex is friends with both of them and taylor is friends with jordan. 

The performance and structural trade-offs for a graph emerge when it comes to printing the network and searching the entire network. While it may be very efficient and easy for the network to add friends and represent the relationships between each, the issue would then come to printing it. To print the network it is required to iterate through every person and their adjacency list. It would be fine for a small network but if it grows it can be more and more expensive.
